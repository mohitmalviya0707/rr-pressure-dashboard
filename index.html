      How it works

      1. Clean the data. IPL and official T20I only (2022–26). No-result matches, DLS games without a revised target and super overs removed.
      2. Win probability. A ball-by-ball Markov model, built separately for IPL and T20I, gives each side's chance of winning from any score, wickets and balls left. Tested on held-out matches: chase AUC .
      3. Pressure. For every ball, how much could the result swing? The top 25% of balls by that swing are pressure balls, leaving out games already decided.
      4. Performance vs expectation. Each run and wicket is compared with an average player in the same format, innings, over, wickets and pressure band, adjusted for the opposition. A wicket is worth its run value at that moment.
      5. Fair comparison. Small samples are pulled toward average (empirical Bayes). Eligibility: at least 120 pressure balls and 8 matches.
      6. Rating. 60% pressure level, 25% match-to-match consistency, 15% lift over own usual level (try it in the Model lab).
      7. Stress test. The full model was re-run under 600 alternative settings. The final top 10s are the players who stay near the top most often, weighting each setting by how repeatable it proved.
      8. Audit. Separate code recomputed the whole pipeline. checks were executed and all passed.

      Limits

      • The main pressure definition is chase-heavy; batting-first pressure is covered by the alternative settings.
      • Size of wides and byes is not recorded, so bowler economy is approximate. Runs off the bat, balls and wickets are exact.
      • Repeatability is real but modest (ρ ), so ranks a few places apart are statistically similar.
      • Fakhar Zaman, Mohammad Nawaz and Abrar Ahmed are ranked on the data, but Pakistan players have not played the IPL since 2008.

      Only summary results are published here. The ball-by-ball competition data is not included.
